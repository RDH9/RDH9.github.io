Subnetting Finally Clicked — Here's the Method That Worked

Subnetting is the thing everyone says is the hardest part of learning networking, and honestly, they're right — until you find the right mental model. Here's the method that made it click for me.

The core trick: mask + block size = 256

Every subnet mask has a corresponding block size, and the two always add up to 256 in whichever octet you're working in. For example:

CIDRMask (last relevant octet)Block Size
/2619264
/2722432
/2824016
/292488

Once you have the block size, you just list boundaries by repeatedly adding it: 0, 32, 64, 96... and find which range your address falls into.

The trap: when the "interesting octet" isn't the last one

The part that actually trips people up is when the subnet mask isn't a clean /24 or smaller — like a /22 or /20, where the block size applies to the third octet instead of the fourth. When that happens, the usable host range spans across multiple values of the last octet.

172.20.100.5/22
Mask: 255.255.252.0 -> block size 4 in the third octet
Boundaries: 100, 104
Subnet: 172.20.100.0/22
Usable range: 172.20.100.1 - 172.20.103.254
Broadcast: 172.20.103.255

Notice the usable range rolls through .100, .101, .102, and .103 — not just within one value of the third octet. That's the part that needs a few reps to feel natural.

Practice makes it automatic

After enough repetition — dozens of problems, mixing block sizes, mixing which octet is "interesting" — it stops being math and starts being pattern recognition. That's really the whole goal: by exam day, you shouldn't be doing arithmetic, you should just recognize the shape of the problem.
